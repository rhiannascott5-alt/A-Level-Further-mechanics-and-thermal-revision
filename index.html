Uniform circular motion

Circular motion

Watch the velocity and acceleration vectors as the mass goes round. Switch to a vertical circle to see why the string tension changes between the top and the bottom.

■ velocity · ■ acceleration / resultant force

Key equations

ω = v/r = 2πfa = v²/r = ω²rF = mv²/r = mω²r

Breaking it down

  1. Speed is constant but the direction of velocity keeps changing, so velocity changes. The object is accelerating.
  2. The acceleration, and so the resultant force, points towards the centre. This is the centripetal force.
  3. Centripetal force is not a new force. It is the resultant of real forces: tension, friction, gravity, the normal reaction.
  4. The force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work and the speed doesn't change.
  5. In a vertical circle, weight helps at the top and opposes at the bottom: Ttop = mv²/r − mg, Tbottom = mv²/r + mg.
  6. The string stays taut at the top only if v² ≥ gr. Below that, gravity alone is more than enough to provide the centripetal force.

Common traps

  • Drawing "centripetal force" as an extra force on a free-body diagram.
  • Saying there is an outward "centrifugal" force. In the ground frame there isn't one.
  • Revolutions per minute to rad s⁻¹: multiply by 2π, then divide by 60.
  • In a vertical circle, using the same speed at the top and the bottom. Energy is conserved, so the speed is lower at the top.

Try this: in the vertical circle, reduce the speed at the bottom until the status says the string goes slack. Check that vtop² is just under gr.

Oscillations

Simple harmonic motion

A mass on a spring on a frictionless surface. The graphs show displacement, velocity and acceleration against time; the bars show how energy moves between kinetic and elastic potential.

x · v · a against time

Key equations

a = −ω²xx = A cos ωtv = ±ω√(A² − x²)vmax = ωAamax = ω²AT = 2π√(m/k)T = 2π√(l/g)

Breaking it down

  1. Definition of SHM: acceleration is proportional to displacement from equilibrium and always directed towards equilibrium (a = −ω²x).
  2. At the extremes: x = ±A, v = 0, |a| is maximum. At equilibrium: x = 0, |v| is maximum, a = 0.
  3. Phase: velocity is a quarter cycle (π/2) ahead of displacement. Acceleration is in antiphase (π) with displacement.
  4. The period does not depend on amplitude. This is what makes SHM isochronous.
  5. Energy swaps between kinetic and potential twice per cycle. Total energy = ½kA² stays constant without damping.
  6. The pendulum formula only works for small angles (under about 10°), where sin θ ≈ θ.

Common traps

  • Using x = A sin ωt when timing starts at maximum displacement. Use cos when x = A at t = 0.
  • Calculator in degrees for x = A cos ωt. ωt is in radians.
  • A mass-spring period does not depend on g. A pendulum period does not depend on mass.
  • KE is maximum twice per cycle, so energy graphs have half the period of the x graph.

Try this: change the amplitude and check the period readout. Then quadruple the mass. What happens to T?

Forced vibrations

Damping and resonance

A driver shakes the top of a spring with a fixed small amplitude. Change the driving frequency and the damping to see the amplitude response and the phase lag.

driver (top) · oscillator (bottom) · response curve

Key ideas

resonance: fdriving = fnaturalat resonance the oscillator lags by π/2

Breaking it down

  1. Free vibration: no external force after release; it oscillates at its natural frequency. Forced vibration: a periodic driving force makes it oscillate at the driving frequency.
  2. Resonance: when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency, energy transfer is most efficient and amplitude is greatest.
  3. Phase: well below resonance the oscillator moves in phase with the driver. At resonance it lags by π/2. Well above, it lags by π (antiphase).
  4. More damping: lower peak amplitude, a broader peak, and the peak moves slightly below the natural frequency.
  5. Light damping: amplitude decays slowly, period unchanged. Critical damping: returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without oscillating (car suspension). Overdamping: returns more slowly, no oscillation.

Common traps

  • Saying the oscillator vibrates at its natural frequency when forced. It vibrates at the driving frequency.
  • Forgetting that heavy damping also shifts the peak to a lower frequency.
  • Mixing up critical and heavy (over) damping.

Try this: set damping to 0.05 and sweep the frequency through 1.00. Watch the phase lag swing from 0° to 180°.

Ideal gases and kinetic theory

Ideal gases

Nitrogen molecules in a container. Change temperature, volume and amount of gas, and watch the pressure. The graph below shows pressure against Celsius temperature at the current volume, extrapolated to absolute zero.

gas: nitrogen, M = 0.028 kg mol⁻¹

Key equations

pV = nRT = NkTpV = ⅓Nm(crms)²½m(crms)² = 3/2 kT = 3RT/2NAW = pΔV

Breaking it down

  1. Temperatures must be in kelvin: T(K) = θ(°C) + 273. Absolute zero (0 K) is where an ideal gas would have zero pressure and its molecules minimum kinetic energy.
  2. The gas laws: Boyle (pV constant at constant T), Charles (V/T constant at constant p), pressure law (p/T constant at constant V).
  3. Pressure from molecules: molecules hit the wall and change momentum. Force = rate of change of momentum. Pressure = total force ÷ area.
  4. Hotter gas: molecules move faster, so they hit the walls more often and with bigger momentum changes. Both raise pressure.
  5. Mean kinetic energy of a molecule depends only on T, not on the type of gas. Heavier molecules move more slowly at the same T.
  6. Assumptions: large number of molecules in random motion; molecular volume negligible; elastic collisions; collision time negligible; no forces between molecules except during collisions.

Common traps

  • Using °C in pV = nRT. Doubling 20 °C to 40 °C does not double the pressure.
  • Mixing n (moles, with R) and N (molecules, with k).
  • Mass of one molecule = M / NA. M must be in kg mol⁻¹.
  • crms is not the mean speed. Square the speeds, average, then square-root.

Try this: double T and watch the pressure. Then put T back and halve V. Compare the two pressure changes.

Thermal energy transfer

Specific and latent heat

Ice at −20 °C is heated at constant power until all of it has boiled away. Press Play to run the heating curve; change the mass and the heater power to see how each stage's time changes.

temperature against time · no heat loss

Key equations

Q = mcΔθQ = mlQ = Pt
cice = 2100cwater = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹lf = 3.34×10⁵lv = 2.26×10⁶ J kg⁻¹

Breaking it down

  1. Internal energy is the sum of the randomly distributed kinetic and potential energies of the molecules.
  2. On the sloping parts, energy raises the kinetic energy of the molecules, so temperature rises. Use Q = mcΔθ.
  3. On the flat parts (melting, boiling), energy raises potential energy by breaking bonds. Temperature stays constant. Use Q = ml.
  4. Vaporisation takes far more energy than melting: the molecules have to separate completely.
  5. For a continuous flow heater, use mass per second: P = (m/t)cΔθ.
  6. For mixing problems, energy lost by the hot object = energy gained by the cold one (if no heat loss).

Common traps

  • Missing a stage. Ice at −10 °C to water at 20 °C needs three calculations: warm the ice, melt it, warm the water.
  • Thinking temperature is rising during a change of state.
  • Heat losses make an experimental c come out too high, since more energy was supplied than the block absorbed.

Try this: compare the time to melt the ice with the time to boil the water away. Which latent heat is bigger, and by how much?
